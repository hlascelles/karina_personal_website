Eating is something I do every day, so might as well do it in a way that contributes to my wellbeing instead of detracting from it.
I want my food to make me happy, to contain things my body needs and to be non-detrimental to my health. 

### Goal #1 - eat food that contains things my body needs

Some things my body needs in large quantities, the macros, and many others in small quantities, the micros. 
The macros are carbs, fats, protein and fiber.

**Protein**  
These days, in 2025, there is a big fuss about protein.
Protein cannot be stored in the body, so there are no leftovers from yesterday - you have to consumme enough of it every day. 
But if you consume more protein than your body can absorb, you create more work for your kidneys, so consuming double or triple the amount needed is not completely harmless.
I delved into the science of how much protein per day is ideal, my conclusion - the order of magnitude is 1g per kilogram of body weight, but it is complicated and any good recommendation comes with at least 37 asterisks. 

For a rough comparison (all numbers are very approximmate), 1 chicken breast has 40g of protein (178g with 22.5% protein), the same volume of cooked chickpeas has 22g of protein (250g with 8.8% protein), a 225g block of tofu has 38g of protein (17%), and a 225g block of paneer has 49g of protein (22% protein).
Here is a rough estimate of the protein content in my favourite vegetarian meals: 
[Palak paneer](https://karina.io/kitchen/palak-paneer/) has 47g protein - 33g from paneer (150g with 22% protein), 7.5g from wholegrain rice (100g with 7.5% protein) and 6.5g from spinach (250g with 2.6% protein). 
[Pasta with parsley and walnut pesto](https://karina.io/kitchen/parsley-walnut-pesto-pasta/) has 34g protein - 19.8g from pasta (137g with 14.5% protein), 9.1g from cheese (35g with 26% protein) and 5.1g from walnuts (35g with 14.6% protein). 
[Lentils in tahini sauce served with quinoa](https://karina.io/kitchen/quinoa-lentil-tahini-goodness/) has 27g protein - 11.7g from lentils (50g with 23.4% protein), 6g from tahini (25g with 24% protein), 4g from feta (25g with 16.5% protein), 3g from quinoa (70g with 4.3% protein) and 2.3g from nutritional yeast flakes (5g with 47% protein). 
[Courgette fritters with potatoes](https://karina.io/kitchen/courgette-fritters/) has 24g protein - 8g from feta (50g with 16.5% protein), 7g from courgettes (350g with 2% protein), 5g from high-protein yoghurt (50g with 10.3% protein), 4g from potatoes (250g with 1.7% protein).

And for my favourite vegan meals: 
[Mushrooms with bean mash](https://karina.io/kitchen/mushrooms-bean-mash/) has 43g of protein - 35.7g from cooked beans (350g with 10.2% protein) and 7.3g from mushrooms (300g with 2.45% protein). 
[Chickpeas, beetroot and kale](https://karina.io/kitchen/) has 27g of protein - 13g from cooked chickpeas (150g with 8.8% protein), 7g from tahini (30g with 24% protein), 4g from beetroot (225g with 1.7% protein) and 3g from kale (100g with 3.2% protein). 
[Cauliflower with Romesco sauce](https://karina.io/kitchen/cauliflower-romesco-sauce/) has 18g of protein - 7g from buckwheat (65g with 11.1% protein), 6g from cauliflower (300g with 2% protein) and 5g from almonds (25g with 21.2% protein). 
[Pineapple red Thai curry](https://karina.io/kitchen/pineapple-red-thai-curry/) has 16g of protein - 7.5g from wholegrain rice (100g with 7.5% protein), 6.5g from cashews (38g with 17.4% protein) and 2g from green beans (100g with 2% protein).

So if I have 2 big meals per day, I average about 1.5 chicken breast worth of protein, which is on the order of magnitude of what my body needs. 
I don't count my protein intake (especially because I don't know how much my body needs on any given day), but I find it helpful to look at the main protein sources in a handful of my faivorite meals. 
A lot of my protein comes from dairy (which is something I would like to change) and pasta (mine is 14.5% protein and I can eat a lot of it). 
Apart from eating legumes (beans, chickpeas, lentils, mung beans, peas, soy beans as edamame and tofu) daily, I'd like to diversify my consumption of wholegrains to include, apart from just rice and quinoa, more buckwheat, millet, wild rice, barley, rye, sorghum, amaranth, teff, fonio, coix seeds and whatever else I can find. 
Vegetables average at 2% protein, but I can eat 300g+ of them them. 
Whole nuts, peanut butter and tahini are 15-24% protein, but I usually don't consume more than 40g of them in a meal. 
Pumpkin seeds (30% protein) and nutritional yeast flakes (47% protein) I eat in even smaller amounts, but I should keep adding them to any dish that can take them.

**Fibre**  
Fibre comes from plants. 
The less processed they are, the more fibre you'll get. 
Think fruits instead of fruit juice, salads with whole grains instead of with pasta or bread (flour-based products), whole tomatoes cooked into a sauce instead of sauce from a jar. 
Unlike protein, you can't really eat too much fibre, so there is no need to count - just try to eat as much of it as possible. 

**Micronutrients**  
Our bodies need a lot of different micros.
The most fun strategy for getting enough of all of them is eating a diverse diet. 
Before refrigiration and supermarkets, diversity was enforced by seasons. 
Nowdays, living in a big city, I have no concept of when courgettes are in season - they are always on a supermarket shelve. 
And I know what to do with them - I might eat them fresh in a salad, cook them into a risotto, a soup or a Thai curry, or eat them fried in a sandwich or fried into fritters. 
Instead of getting radishes for my salad, asparagus for risotto, beetroot for soup, pumpkin for Thai curry and mushrooms for sandwisches, courgette is my lazy default. 
Having healthy defaults is good; buying courgettes every time I'm too tired to be creative is better than ordering a pizza.
But when I can, I try to cook with plants I'm less familiar with - celeriac, fennel, okra, radicchio, etc.
Luckily there is no shortage of online recipes, and with image recognition even not knowing the name of the vegetable is no longer an exuse.

I'm mindful of fake diversity. 
Bulgur, couscous, farro, freekeh, fregola, orzo, pasta, polenta, semolina, spelt and sourdough bread - sounds like a diverse list of ingredients, but these are all types of wheat and products made from wheat flour. 
Edamame beans, tofu, tempeh and vegan "chicken" pieces all come from soya plants. 
Breakfast options might include pastries, butter and jam on toast, crepes, pancakes and waffles - all permutations of wheat flour, sugar and butter. 
Wheat tortillas, meat, bell peppers and cheese can be made into a dozen Mexican dishes - keep tortillas flat for tlayudas, empalmes (two tlayudas on top of each other) and sincronizadas (empalmes with bigger tortillas), fold tortillas in half for tacos and quesadilla (tacos with more cheese), roll up tortillas into enchiladas, flautas (fried enchiladas) and burritos (bigger tortillas), or cut tortillas into triangles for chilaquiles, etc.
Instead of focusing on the names of the dishes, I try to focus on the diversity of ingredients first and then combine them into dishes, traditional or experimental.

Avoiding food waste, both in my kitchen and before the products get to my kitchen, is another great strategy for hitting targets for my micros. 
I don't peal fruits and vegetables unless absolutely necessary - there are a lot of good micros in the skins. 
Unpeeled ginger is great in a curry paste and unpeeled kiwi is great in a fruit salad, though many of my friends had to lead by example before I tried the latter. 
I recently discovered how much I like carrot leave pesto and sautéed beetroot leaves, so I try to get into a habit of looking for recipes online before binning plant body parts that I don't know how to use. 
Since there is an infinite number of online recipes to choose from, I avoid recipes that call for red lentils (peeled green lentils), white rice (wholegrain rice with the bran coating removed), white pepper (peeled black peppercorns), etc. 
It took energy to produce these skins and bran coatings, they contain a lot of micros, and it takes energy to remove them, so I'd rather eat them.

Our tongue can only register 5 tastes: sweet, sour, bitter, salty, & umami.

To sweet beetroot, add bitter flavours like grapefruit and walnuts.
