After finishing my PhD, I was eager to get my hands on messy real world problems. Back then the waste management sector seemed to have the highest ratio of GHG emissions to the amount of attention the sector was getting. And since working on landfills didn't sound like a glamorous career choice, I decided this was exactly the area where I could make the most impact. 

Through hundreds of conversations with relevant stakeholders, I established that everyone knew what the problems were and everyone had ideas about what should be done; and yet the World Bank's [2018 report](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/d3f9d45e-115f-559b-b14f-28552410e90a) showed no improvement on [2012](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/1a464650-9d7a-58bb-b0ea-33ac4cd1f73c). Thinking of waste flows as Sankey diagrams, it made sense for me to work on improving a waste management system as a whole, instead of focussing on a particular node such as collecting recyclable plastic or removing waste from the ocean. I imagined creating a system accelerator - a framework in which capacities of different stakeholders could be combined in new ways to create a functional system, a waste management system in this particular case, to achieve goals, such as reduction of GHG emissions, that are not aligned with financial incentives. I started by working with different types of stakeholders (waste management companies, recycling startups, non-profits, city governments, national governments, UN agencies and universities) to understand their capacities and limitations. 

This was an exciting journey. I learned a lot about politics, business models and behavioural science, as well as about polymer chemistry and myself. Working independently, I could not achieve the momentum required to run a system accelerator I imagined. I had to build a team, but I lacked the skills to do that. I took a career break to focus on personal goals.

Now I am looking to join a team to work on something that requires more momentum than a single person can generate. I am open to ideas, especially ones that sound a bit crazy. 

The questions I am asking myself are whether the work I'll be doing has to be climate related? whether I can make more impact by leveraging my STEM or non-STEM skills? and what kind of people do I want to work with? If I choose to work on something exciting but unrelated to climate, that would imply an assumption that either a climate disaster is not likely or that somebody else will prevent it. If I'm being honest with myself, I can't afford these assumptions. [How to Avoid a Climate Disaster](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/52908942-how-to-avoid-a-climate-disaster) (2021) book by Bill Gates convinced me that working on reducing GHG emissions, from the waste management sector or any other sector, is futile. If all the restrictions imposed by the covid-19 pandemic resulted in 4.5% reduction of GHG emissions, it is unimaginable that we could achieve a 100% reduction through some combination of policy, business models and voluntary action. In contrast, accelerating development and/or adoption of clean energy sources is a plausible strategy.

I believe I could make the most impact by applying my STEM skills and ways of thinking to problems that are typically thought of as requiring non-STEM skills.

The three main sources of friction between me and other people working on climate are the misalignment of goals, the disagreement over the concept of a just transition, and the unwillingness of people to be honest with themselves. My goal is to maintain a liveable planet. This is not the same as improving human welfare. When it comes to trade-offs, the maths for humans-first and planet-first approaches is very different. People get frustrated with me and say "but that would hurt the very people you are trying to help". I am not trying to help people. Not because I'm a particularly malicious person, but because people have agency. People on a liveable planet will find ways to help themselves. 

This human-centric approach means that people working on climate consider work on air pollution (it kills more people than the rising temperatures) and healthcare (a child won't enjoy a liveable planet if it is dead) equally important. And I am not saying it is less important. I'm saying that climate seems like a hard problem and we've not made that much progress on it yet, so I think we should approach it with a ruthless focus exclusively on climate. Not everyone should be working on climate, but we need some people whose work is focused exclusively on climate, without being distracted by other human welfare topics.

A lot of people working on climate have a hidden justice agenda. This baffles me. Everyone agrees that climate is a hard problem. Some say we can't do anything about it because it is too hard. Some say it is too late to act, it was hard to solve before but now we are too far down the downward spiral and the problem has become impossible to solve. And then suddenly a "just transition" appears on the agenda. So now we are not just solving climate but also working on resolving income (and gender and whatnot) inequalities? I've not heard of any just transitions in human history. We've had transitions to agriculture, The Industrial Revolution and transitions to a digital age, and all of these transitions created winners and losers. How can anyone think that we can both avert a climate disaster and also achieve the first ever just transition in human history at the same time?

I don't hate rich people, men, big companies, oil companies or capitalism. I don't hate anyone, and this alienates me from many in the climate sector.

When a person motivated purely by money tells me "we do it this way because it is more profitable", I feel like they are being honest. I don't particularly like them, but I trust them. Talking to people who work on climate, I often feel like they are not being honest. The people who started with a noble aim (like solving climate problems) and now they are trying to succeed in the economic system while acting to defy the economic pressures of this system. The stories they tell themselves are different from what they tell their investors. They are not being honest neither with the investors, nor with themselves, let alone me, a random stranger.

To summarise, I am looking to join a team working on averting a climate disaster. Probably on something related to accelerating development and/or adoption of clean energy sources. I am looking for people who are focused on climate (not to improve human lives, but to maintain a liveable planet on which people can live), who don't hate anyone, and who can honestly acknowledge the tensions between climate goals and economic incentives and find ways to navigate those tensions. People who have the audacity to develop new economic incentives inside the current economic system, instead of saying "let's abolish capitalism" and "just stop oil" without having any coherent vision for the future. People who are attacking climate problems with the ferociousness of a greedy businessman chasing profits, not with loving care of a charity worker, people who are driven by facts not emotions, people who embrace complexity instead of pretending it doesn't exist.
